# Field notes: finding a room in Vancouver as a newcomer

I arrived in Vancouver at the end of August 2026. I had a temporary room in a share
house until September 30 and no Canadian credit history. These are the notes from the
search that followed, in the order things happened. Every rule in [the
skill](skill/SKILL.md) comes from one of these moments.

Names, addresses and my own numbers are left out. Prices are what the market showed
in September 2026.

## 1. What I was looking for

- A real bedroom in a room share. Whole units were out from the start: they run
  credit checks and want proof of income, and I had neither.
- Move in on October 1, month to month or a short fixed term. I did not know where my
  job would be, and Vancouver tech is spread across downtown, Mount Pleasant and
  Burnaby.
- A kitchen I could actually cook in. What I cook smells (pan-fried pork belly, stews,
  ramen), so that became a question to ask before signing, not a detail.
- Quiet. I had no idea yet how much that one would end up deciding.

## 2. What a room really costs

The first lesson was arithmetic. Rent is not the price of a room. The price is rent +
utilities + the transit pass the address forces.

- **Buses ignore zones.** A monthly pass limits zones on SkyTrain and SeaBus only, so a
  bus commute costs a 1-zone pass from anywhere in Metro Vancouver.
- **Walking beats every pass.** If the daily anchor (for me, a place downtown) is a
  walk of 35 minutes or less, the room needs only rain-day fares, about CAD 50 a month.
- **Utilities are worth about CAD 60 a month.** That is more than the gap between Zone 1
  and Zone 2.

So a walkable CAD 967 room, a Zone 1 CAD 900 room and a Zone 2 CAD 861 room all cost
about the same. Then the market surprised me. The West End is walkable to downtown, but
its median room was CAD 1,200. Only a few West End rooms under about CAD 1,000 beat a
Commercial Drive room at CAD 800 plus a pass.

Measured over 768 listings, more money bought three things and little else:

| What | Where it started |
|---|---|
| A bathroom with a shower, not shared | about CAD 1,000 in Vancouver |
| A private entrance | about CAD 1,050 |
| A walk to downtown instead of a commute | about CAD 1,000 in the West End |

Going far out saved almost nothing at the median, about CAD 850 in Surrey against
875 in Vancouver. What changed was the price of a private bathroom: about 700 in Surrey.
The suburbs do not buy a cheaper room. They buy a better room for the same money.

## 3. Where the rooms are

No single site has the market.

- **Craigslist** has the deepest inventory and the worst location data. Half the posts
  say only "Vancouver".
- **Facebook Marketplace** has the best data: street address, bed and bath counts, and
  how many people already live there.
- **Facebook groups** have posts that never reach Craigslist. Reading them needs
  membership, so I joined about sixteen. Searching works; scrolling the feed does not.
- **The Korean community boards** (UvanU, the Naver working-holiday cafes, Vanchosun)
  turn over the fastest, and they are the only place men-only rooms were advertised.
  They also carry the most "living-room share" posts priced like bedrooms.
- **Kijiji and Rentals.ca** are small but different. Rentals.ca had private-bathroom
  rooms cheaper than Craigslist's tier.

What I learned about timing:

- An ad renewed last night is a live landlord. On the same afternoon I sent two
  enquiries. The fresh ad offered a viewing within three hours. The ad untouched for a
  month answered "taken, forgot to remove it".
- Late in the month, the pool for the 1st is leftovers. The posts worth chasing are
  from the last 48 hours, and they deserve a message the same day.

**The reverse channel works.** In the "room wanted" groups, posts asking for a room got
8 to 11 replies within hours, many with real offers.

## 4. Reading a listing

The card lies by omission. The body is where the deal-breakers live.

- **"Female only" sits in the first line of the body**, under a neutral title. One
  evening it was four of six. The opposite mistake is just as real: a room shared with
  two women and no stated rule is open to anyone.
- **A den is not a bedroom**, and neither is a partitioned living room. Someone sleeping
  in the living room changes the whole unit.
- **No photo of the room means no viewing.** Anyone can photograph the living room. Good
  photos are not proof either: scams usually have them.
- **"Furnished" is a photo with a bed in it.** One room I almost shortlisted had a body
  that read like a furnished condo. All four photos showed an empty unit.
- **"Working professional or student preferred" is a preference. "Credit check" is a
  gate.** I could not pass the gates yet, so I skipped them and prepared an answer for
  the preferences. Some posts welcomed newcomers on work permits outright.

## 5. Scams, deposits and money

The pinned warnings on every Korean board describe the same pattern: a below-market
room, and a deposit asked for before anyone sees it.

- **The tell is "no viewing", not "deposit talk".** Korean posts often say "first
  deposit gets the room". That alone is not a scam if a viewing is offered.
- **A landlord who cannot show the place is different from one who will not.** One
  operator lived abroad and had the current housemates show the room. That is the
  opposite of "I am overseas, send the deposit and I will mail the keys".
- **Two posts with the same photos are a question to answer first.** One pair turned out
  to be a couple each advertising the same room. It could just as well have been someone
  copying photos.
- **A "deposit" that works like a reservation fee is not a deposit.** At one viewing the
  host offered no written agreement and wanted the deposit as a booking fee. With no
  paper, there is nothing to stand on if it goes wrong.

## 6. The first viewing: I was being interviewed

The first room I saw was a high-floor condo room with a great view and a host who lived
there. I loved it. I barely asked anything. The host kept asking whether I was a
student, whether I had a job, and about my bank account. I had nothing ready to show. I
walked out sure the host had cooled on me, and the host never answered my follow-up.

The AI's first explanation was that its viewing guide had too many questions. That was
wrong: I had not used them. The real problem was money. A job-seeking tenant is checked
for funds first. Since then every viewing has started the same way:

1. **Proof of funds ready on the phone**, enough to cover the minimum term, account
   numbers hidden.
2. **"Are you a student? Do you have a job?" answered in three sentences**: my experience,
   where the job search stands that day, and rent paid from savings I can show.
3. **One line that fits the host's life.** That host worked early shifts, and "I'd keep
   mornings and nights quiet" would have cost nothing.
4. **A thank-you the same evening** that repeats one detail from the conversation.

The lesson for the AI: ask what happened at a viewing before explaining why it went
badly.

## 7. Noise: finding out what actually bothered me

I kept saying "quiet" without knowing what I meant. The temporary room I was living in
became the measuring stick. The question was never "is this room quiet?" It was "is this
room better than where I am now, and at what?"

What I actually heard at 1 a.m. was muffled voices from the next bedroom: audible, not
intelligible. That narrowed everything.

- **The building code does not cover that wall.** BC requires STC 50 between dwelling
  units, not between bedrooms inside one. A brand-new house and a fifty-year-old one can have
  the same bedroom wall. "Newer" helps with the suite below and with nobody overhead, nothing more.
- **Layout beats wall performance.** What fixed it was a room with no bedroom on the other
  side of the wall. A hall, a closet or a bathroom in between works. Two bathrooms in
  between works even better.
- **The door is half of it.** A hollow door with a gap underneath has no real rating. So
  where the door opens to (a quiet entrance, a stair landing, an open kitchen shared by
  five) matters as much as the walls.
- **View in the evening.** In the afternoon a share house is empty. At 7pm everyone is
  cooking and showering.
- **Test it instead of guessing.** Close the door and run a phone sound meter for a
  minute: 30 to 35 dB is fine, 45 dB means words come through. Knock the wall and the
  door. Ask whoever shows the room to talk normally in the next bedroom for thirty
  seconds. And ask a housemate, "can you hear the next room?" They know, and an owner
  will not volunteer it.

## 8. Who lives there, and who is the landlord

- **"Three of us" can mean one suite of a much bigger house.** BC Assessment's free
  property page gives bedroom and bathroom counts and the floor area of each level. In
  one day three addresses turned out to hold far more bedrooms than the ads' headcounts
  implied, and one had a second building the ad never mentioned. For another, the
  mailboxes in the street photo showed the house was divided into separate suites, which
  changed how many people would share the kitchen.
- **A flowerbed at eye level means below grade.** I logged one room as "ground floor"
  from its window photo. BC Assessment showed a one-storey house with a finished basement
  and the owner living upstairs.
- **The law depends on who you sign with.** The Residential Tenancy Act does not apply if
  you share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner. If you are a tenant's roommate, you are
  an "occupant" with no standing under it at all. An operator who places roommates but
  lives elsewhere is a landlord under it, whatever the paper is called.
- **A downtown condo viewing showed all of this at once**: four people, someone living in
  the living room, a shared bathroom for three, no written agreement, and the deposit as a
  booking fee. The view was the best I saw, and I still walked away. My new rule was to
  get three things in writing before booking any viewing: the total headcount, whether
  anyone lives in the living room or den, and whether an agreement will be signed.

## 9. Criteria that kept moving

I rewrote my criteria five times in two weeks, and once threw everything out and started
over. That is normal. Treating yesterday's filter as final is the mistake.

- **What I said I would tolerate was not what I wanted.** I said a long ride was fine for
  a quiet room, so the AI ranked suburban rooms first. I disliked the whole batch. Ten of
  the thirteen places I had picked myself were on or beside the downtown peninsula.
- **Fifteen knockouts leave "nothing wrong", not "good".** Keep the knockouts to facts
  (move-in date, a bed, headcount, nobody in the living room) and score the rest.
- **Check the pattern of what you actually click.** It is better evidence than what you
  say.

## 10. Three finalists in one day

On one Sunday I viewed three rooms that had all passed the bedroom-wall test.

**Room A**, a big room on a floor shared by three, shown by a tenant. The other rooms
were well away from mine, but one wall touched the living room and the door sat next to
the stairs. The host said "just tell me a day before you leave". The AI logged that as
one-day eviction and made it the room's headline weakness. The host meant flexibility for
me, and I had to point that out. The written contract, which came later, showed a separate
gap: it had no notice period for the host's side.

**Room B**, a whole ground-floor suite with an official tenancy agreement, so real tenant
protection and nobody on the other side of any wall. I paid about CAD 15 for an LTSA title
search that evening. The registered owner had a different given name from the person I
was about to pay. It was almost certainly family, and the rest of the title was clean. The
landlord also offered a lower rent if I signed the next day, and wanted the first month
paid before the agreement was written. The right order is to fill in the blanks
together, then transfer, then sign. Between the seven-month fixed term (a tenant cannot
end one early), the payment order and my disappointment over the name, B dropped out.
The seven months weighed most. Spending that much to live alone also felt like a luxury
this early, when I had not even tried a room share.

**Room C**, an upper-floor room in a house run by an operator who lived abroad. The
housemates showed it. Two bathrooms sat between my room and the next bedroom, but the door
opened onto the shared open kitchen and living room. It had
house rules and it felt lived in. The housemate's account of the contract was more
convincing than anything an owner could have said.

My comparison tables had covered acoustics, contract and money. They had no column for
house rules or a house that feels lived in. That night I chose C.

## 11. Deciding, and then the market decided

The next morning I viewed one more room: a larger room in a house with the owner
upstairs, rented out by a chef who included groceries in the rent. Rent and deposit were
due on move-in day, and the minimum was five months. I came back unable to think straight about it, and asked
for an objective review. That review is where the flowerbed turned into a basement.

Now I had three live options and could not choose. A comparison table sat for a day
without a decision. What worked was an interview: four rounds of short questions, up to five each.
Each question said which room its answer would favour, and each asked about a real moment:
the sound at bedtime, eating dinner alone, the rainy commute. The decisive answer came
sideways. Asked why one room appealed to me, I described a different one from start to
finish. The interview ranked the chef's house first, Room A second, and Room C out.

Then the market moved.

- The next morning Room A's host had found someone else.
- The chef had not even read my message by late morning, and something about it felt off.
- A new listing I had planned to view that evening was two people advertising the same
  room.

That evening I signed with Room C, the room I had first chosen two nights earlier. Two
things made that safe. Every finalist had been checked to the same depth. And I had kept
C open by telling the operator, honestly, that I needed one more day.

One thing I got wrong about C. Part of its appeal was daily English with the couple who
lived there, and they turned out to live in the very room I was taking. I heard they were
moving out and crossed English off the list. They were moving into the master room, so
they stayed in the house. **Moving out of this room is not moving out of the house: ask
who will live where after you move in.**

## 12. Contract and money

- **Read the blanks, not the boilerplate**: rent, term, what is included, the deposit,
  the notice clause, whose name is the landlord. I asked for three additions: a notice
  period for the operator's side, the private toilet in writing, and consent to fit a door
  lock. None made it into the final document. I got the private toilet and the payee's
  name confirmed in chat and saved screenshots, which is the next best thing.
- **Three names must match**: the owner on title, the landlord on the agreement, and the
  person receiving the money. Interac e-Transfer shows the recipient's registered legal
  name only when they use Autodeposit. When it showed, it matched.
- **Deposit at signing, first month's rent as late as the house allows.** Same total,
  less money at risk before keys. The house rule was rent a few days before month-end, so
  I asked to pay October's only after I had been added to the residents' group chat, and
  the operator agreed. Under a tenancy the deposit is at most half a month.
- **Check the payee's email twice.** The address I was given had a typo, and the
  Autodeposit name check was what confirmed I had the right person.
- **A room without a lock is a real step down.** Under the Act a tenant cannot change a
  lock without the landlord's written consent, so ask in writing. Meanwhile, keep the
  passport and any drive with private files in a lockable box.
- **Signing a PDF on a Mac**: open it in Preview, add a trackpad signature with Markup,
  type into the blanks, then print to PDF so the result is flattened and cannot be edited.

## 13. What I would tell the next person

1. Price the total, never the rent.
2. Search every source, and keep doing it until you sign.
3. Read the body of every post before you judge it.
4. Never pay before you have seen the unit. Fill in the blanks, then pay, then sign.
5. Put the address into BC Assessment the moment you get it.
6. Know whether you will be a tenant or an occupant before you care about the clauses.
7. Find out what noise you actually mind, using the room you live in now.
8. View in the evening, and test the door, not only the wall.
9. Ask a housemate the questions an owner will not answer.
10. Write every message and answer down, with the date, outside the chat.
11. When you cannot choose, stop tabulating and answer questions about your real days.
12. Keep your second choice open until the first one is signed.
