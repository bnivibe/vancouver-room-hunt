# Viewings, noise and rooms

How to judge a room in person when noise is the top criterion, and what to ask
before going at all.

## Before going: only the questions that could stop the trip

Do not ask what the renter will see standing in the room (whether it is above
ground, how it looks, where the washer is). Ask what is invisible on the day and
would waste the trip:

1. The move-in date, the minimum term, and whether an agreement is signed.
2. Who else lives there, and any children, dogs or couples.
3. Whether anyone sleeps in the living room or den, or will be added later.
4. Which space the room shares a wall with, and which floor it is on.
5. What utilities cost, if not included.

Re-fetch and diff the saved listing the day before.

## Grading the building

| Grade | Building |
|---|---|
| A | Concrete high-rise |
| B | A house with nobody above or below, or the top floor of a wood frame |
| C | A middle or lower floor of a wood frame, or a house main floor with a household upstairs |

Identify the building first (BC Assessment: year, storeys, construction). An
unidentified building is graded from photos and marked as a guess.

## What actually wakes someone in a share house

Ranked, worst first:

1. **Footfall from the floor above**, by far the worst in wood frame.
2. A wall against the kitchen or living room.
3. A wall against the stairs.
4. Hallway traffic at night.
5. **Another bedroom**: two sleeping people either side of a wall is the mildest case.

So a top-floor room with its own toilet removes the two biggest sources outright,
and can beat a newer room with people walking overhead.

**Be precise about the building code.** BC requires STC 50 **between dwelling
units**, not between rooms inside one. A recent build guarantees the floor to the
suite below and nothing about the wall to the next bedroom. Muffled speech that is
audible but not intelligible is the signature of an uninsulated 2x4 wall, roughly
STC 33 to 35. What a bigger floor buys is **layout, not wall performance**: a hall,
a closet or a bathroom between two bedrooms, and an ensuite between them is
effectively a double wall.

**The door is half of it, and nobody checks it.** Muffled voices at 1 a.m. are a
barrier that stops the highs and passes the lows. In a share house the largest leak
is almost never the wall: a hollow-core door with a 1 cm undercut has effectively no
rating. Diagnose by ear in two minutes (door gap, shared wall, floor, ceiling vent)
and confirm by rolling a towel against the door bottom and listening for the drop,
which doubles as that night's fix. When every finalist passes the bedroom-wall test,
rank them by **what the door faces**: an entrance nobody passes (best), a stair
landing, or an open kitchen and living room everyone shares (worst).

**Heating is a noise question.** Forced air runs ducts between rooms and suites, and
ducts carry voices. Radiant floor heating has no ducts and no fan, and when utilities
are included a heated winter is the landlord's bill. In-unit laundry removes a
recurring source of people in the renter's space.

## Tests to run at the viewing

- **Prefer an evening viewing.** An afternoon share house is empty; 7pm is when
  everyone cooks and showers, the only time a closed-door test means anything.
- **Sound meter**: door shut, a phone meter for a minute. 30 to 35 dB is quiet enough
  to sleep and take an interview, 40 dB means voices are noticeable, **45 dB and up
  means words are intelligible and the room fails.**
- **Knock the wall** (a loud hollow ring is an uninsulated stud wall) **and the door**
  (hollow ring versus a dull thud); look for light under the closed door and a rubber
  seal on the frame.
- **Ask whoever shows the room to talk normally in the next bedroom for thirty
  seconds** while the renter listens with the door shut.
- **Walk the hall once** and note what each of the room's four walls touches.
- **Calibrate against the current place.** If the renter lives in a share house now,
  measure the current room the same way, late at night and in the evening; every
  later reading then compares with something they already know. Ask what they
  actually hear where they live now, and from which direction.
- **Check the bedroom door lock.** A room without one is a step down nobody puts on
  the checklist; what is at stake is the passport and any drive holding private
  files, more than a laptop carried daily.
- **Ask a housemate**: the kitchen at 8am, laundry queues, how long they have lived
  there, why this room is free, "do you hear the next room?"
- **Pace the room** (an adult stride is about 75 cm) and sit at the desk with a laptop
  open, because that is where a job hunt happens.

## Reading photos

- **Occupied-room photos are evidence of authenticity** (rumpled bedding, a full
  closet), unlike a realtor's empty set, but the furniture in frame may leave with
  the tenant: ask which pieces stay.
- **A phone's wide angle enlarges a room.** Estimate from what is against the walls
  (a dresser about 80 cm, a desk 110 cm, a queen bed 152 x 203 cm).
- **Note what the ad did not mention.** A dog asleep on the sill in the living-room
  photo, after a headcount answer that listed only people, is worth raising.
- **Windows tell the floor**: a living room looking level at a fence and the
  neighbour's ground floor is not on the top floor.

## Master, second and third rooms

On Korean boards the room names are a size ladder, not a bathroom count. The master
is the largest bedroom and usually has an ensuite with a shower; the second and third
descend and usually share. A "second room with a private bathroom" whose fixture is a
toilet and sink (a half bath) is named honestly. Price it accordingly: the
private-bathroom price tier is for rooms with a shower, so a half bath is a shared
bathroom with the morning toilet queue removed, most of the benefit but not the tier.

## Cooking is a contract term

If the renter cooks food that smells (pan-fried pork belly, stews, ramen), ask
before signing whether strong-smelling cooking and pan-frying are fine, whether the
range hood vents outside or only recirculates, and whether cooking has set off the
smoke alarm. Never hide it to win a room; the friction arrives every evening after
move-in. A blocked kitchen costs CAD 150 to 200 a month in food.

## The host phrase bank

Keep a living file of lines for viewings and record, after each one, which lines
were used and how the host reacted, so the bank learns. Only write lines the renter
can stand behind: "I'd love to take it" only if they mean it.
